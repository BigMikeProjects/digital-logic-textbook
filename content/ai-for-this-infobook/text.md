The previous section described how you, the student, are encouraged to use AI in this course. This section is the other half of that transparency: how AI was used to build the Infobook you are reading. Generative tools are woven through nearly every stage of its production, and you deserve to know where and how.

The visual layer comes first. I primarily use models from Anthropic (Claude) and OpenAI to generate the images and interactive infographics that accompany each topic. These are the diagrams you can click on, the animations of signals moving through circuits, and the illustrations that give an abstract idea something concrete to hang on. This is also where most of the production time goes. A first draft of a graphic arrives quickly, but the iteration does not: it is an interactive back-and-forth with the models — adjusting what a graphic shows, how it behaves, what a learner should notice first — with me driving the direction of the visualization toward something that will actually help you learn digital systems. A graphic goes through many rounds before it earns its place on a page.

The teaching itself stays human. With an infographic in hand, I narrate a video over it, walking through the topic the way I would in a lecture: what the circuit does, why it is built that way, where students typically stumble. The explanations, the emphasis, and the judgment about what matters are mine — the AI's role at this stage is to have built the visual I am pointing at.

The written material then grows out of that recorded teaching. AI extracts a transcript from each video, and from the transcript it helps generate the prompts used to flesh out the topic's text description — the pages like the one you are reading now. This ordering is deliberate. The text is not generated from thin air; it is grounded in what was actually taught in the video, then expanded into prose with the structure, notation, worked examples, and review questions that a written reference can carry better than a recording can.

It is worth being candid about the quality of that AI-generated text. My take is that it does a decent job at what this book needs: technical explanations, where there is a definite circuit, a definite truth table, and a right answer the prose either captures or does not. That is a domain where I can check the output against the type of text I want to see in a sold textbook. If I were teaching a writing or literature course — where the voice, the craft, and the originality of the prose are themselves the point — I would be much more hesitant to use this approach. Here, the text is a vehicle for precise ideas, and that is the kind of writing these models often can handle well.

The goal of all of this is a structured curriculum. As the Learning Cycle section argues, videos on their own — however good — arrive as disconnected pieces. The written descriptions are what give the content its shape: they fix an order, connect each topic to its neighbors, and give you a single coherent path from basic switches to working digital systems. AI makes it practical for one instructor to produce all three layers — interactive graphics, narrated video, and structured text — for every topic; the curation, review, and final say on every page remain with me.

If you notice something that seems off — an error, an awkward explanation, a graphic that confuses more than it clarifies — tell me. Every page has the potential to be revised over time, and knowing where the material fails you is exactly the feedback that improves it.
