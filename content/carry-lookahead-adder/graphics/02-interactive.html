The ripple adder is slow for exactly one reason: stage k cannot start until stage k−1 hands it a carry. So ask a different question — what can a column know about its carry before that carry arrives?
QUESTION ONE
Generate — Gi = Ai · Bi
Does this column make a carry all by itself? Only if both bits are 1. Nothing else can produce a carry out of nothing.
QUESTION TWO
Propagate — Pi = Ai ⊕ Bi
If a carry arrives, will this column pass it along? Only if exactly one bit is 1 — then the column's own sum is already 1, and an incoming carry has nowhere to go but out.
Both are computable immediately from the inputs. Neither waits for anything.
Ci+1 = Gi + Pi · Ci “a carry leaves if this column generated one, or if one came in and the column passes it”

Addition sits on the critical path of almost every processor, so the adder is where architects spend area to buy time.
WHERE IT LIVES
Inside the ALU
Every add, every compare, and every address calculation runs through an adder. Shaving its delay shortens the clock period for the whole machine, which is why nobody ships a ripple adder in a datapath.
THE TRADE
Speed bought with area
The lookahead logic computes nothing the ripple adder did not also compute. It just refuses to wait for it. You are paying gates for time — the recurring bargain of digital design.
THE DESCENDANTS
Prefix adders
Push the same idea further and the carry computation becomes a tree. Kogge–Stone and Brent–Kung adders are lookahead taken to its logical end, and are what you will meet in a VLSI course.
TIES BACK TO THE RIPPLE ADDER
Same answer, different wait
A Ripple Carry Adder and this one produce identical sums for every input — verified here over all 512 cases. The only difference is when the answer is ready.
!
Worth saying plainly: the ripple adder is not wrong, and for a few bits it is the sensible choice — less logic, less area, simpler to read. Lookahead earns its cost only when the width gets large enough that the wait starts to hurt.
This block's Verilog idiom: concatenation to build the carry vector
The 4-bit lookahead unit — written structurally, because the structure is the point
module cla4 (input [3:0] a, b, input cin, output [3:0] s, output cout); wire [3:0] g = a & b; // generate wire [3:0] p = a ^ b; // propagate wire c1 = g[0] | (p[0] & cin); wire c2 = g[1] | (p[1] & g[0]) | (p[1] & p[0] & cin); wire c3 = g[2] | (p[2] & g[1]) | (p[2] & p[1] & g[0]) | (p[2] & p[1] & p[0] & cin); assign cout = g[3] | (p[3] & g[2]) | (p[3] & p[2] & g[1]) | (p[3] & p[2] & p[1] & g[0]) | (p[3] & p[2] & p[1] & p[0] & cin); assign s = p ^ {c3, c2, c1, cin}; // all four sum bits at once endmodule
{c3, c2, c1, cin} builds the carry vector, and one XOR produces every sum bit.
!
In practice you would write assign {cout, s} = a + b + cin; and let the synthesiser choose the structure — and it would probably choose something better than this. The explicit version is here because this topic is about the choice. Knowing what the + costs is the point.
