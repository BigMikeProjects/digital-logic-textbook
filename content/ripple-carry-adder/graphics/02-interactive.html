A 4-bit adder is four identical full adders in a row, each handing its carry-out to the next stage's carry-in. That is the whole design. What matters for the rest of this graphic is not the gates inside a stage — it is the three things a stage can do with its carry-in, because that is what decides whether the carry has to keep traveling.

Only a propagating stage has to wait for its carry-in. A stage that kills or generates already knows its carry-out from Ai and Bi alone — so it breaks the chain and the waiting starts over. That single fact is the whole timing story on beat 3.

The modelstated, not assumed
One Δ is the time a carry takes to cross one stage that has to pass it along. A propagating stage cannot produce its carry-out until its carry-in arrives, so it adds a Δ. A killing or generating stage does not wait, so it resets the count.

Worst case = every stage propagating = n·Δ. Notice what that means: the slowest case is not the one with the biggest carry — it is the one where every stage is merely passing a carry along, whether or not a carry ever actually appears.
How wide, how slowthe logic is fine; the time is not
What is Δ worth?tying this to the full-adder topic
Δ is one stage's carry delay, so its value depends on which full adder you built. Full and Half Adders measured the two realizations at a delay of about 3 for the flat two-level (all-NAND) column and about 6 for the one built from two half adders. So 4Δ is roughly 12 to 24 gate transitions for a 4-bit adder — the scaling law above is the same either way, which is exactly why it is written in Δ.

The logic never gets harder as n grows. Only the time does, and it grows linearly. That is the problem the carry-look-ahead adder exists to solve.
