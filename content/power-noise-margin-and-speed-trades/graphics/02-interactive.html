Noise margin shrinks with the supply; noise does not, unless the design cuts it — margin on each side, in volts
Ground bounce, crosstalk and supply ripple come from the board and the package. Every drop in VDD shrinks the margin, so it has to be matched by quieter design: more ground pins, on-chip capacitors, better boards. Lowering the supply is only possible once the noise comes down with it.

Why chips settled where they did — each generation is a new, better car
The car keeps getting better
distance = the swing, VDDweight = the loadengine = the currentclearance = noise marginbumps = noise
  • Beats 1–3 drive one car inside its safe window: it redlines near 1.2 V and stalls toward Vt. Turning its engine down makes every trip slower.
  • Each generation built a better car. Lighter, so it went faster on less fuel and clocks rose. Lower to the ground, so the roads and suspension had to improve: quieter boards, packages and on-chip capacitors.
  • Then the tiny car could not go faster. Vt could not drop without leaking, and packed cars ran too hot. Since about 2005 clocks hold at 3–5 GHz, and chips add more cars (cores) instead.
About 1 V is where most chips sit today, but it is not the answer for every chip. A hearing aid, a phone, a laptop and a server do different jobs, so each one gives up a different corner of the triangle. Beat 5 puts each of them on the knobs.

A third way out: more gates, each one slower — how NVIDIA GPUs and AI chips get their speed
Same work: 2.5 billion operations/sVDDclock, eachpower
  • Splitting the work lets every copy slow down, and a slower clock lets the supply drop, which pays back as the square.
  • The price is silicon: 4 copies need 4× the gates. A GPU has thousands of cores, so it still draws hundreds of watts.
Two copies do the same work for 36% less power; four copies, for 51% less.
Pick the corner you can give up. Apple's chips put the phone and laptop rows on one die: efficiency cores for the light work, performance cores for the heavy work, and DVFS moves each group's supply and clock all day.

Definitions
D or Esc closes
Supply voltage, VDD
The voltage that powers every gate. A logic 1 sits near VDD and a logic 0 near 0 V, so VDD also sets how far apart 0 and 1 are.
Noise margin, NM
How much noise a signal can pick up before the next gate misreads it: NMH = VOH − VIH, NML = VIL − VOL. Here outputs promise 0.25 / 0.75 VDD and inputs need 0.35 / 0.65 VDD (the published 1.2 V levels), so both are 0.1 VDD. See Gate Technologies and Noise Margin.
Gate delay, tpd
The time from an input change to the output change. A gate charges its load with the current its transistors can push; less voltage means much less current. More supply means a bigger swing to cover, but the current grows faster than the swing. Here tpd ∝ VDD / (VDD − Vt)2.
Threshold, Vt
The gate voltage a transistor needs before it starts to conduct (0.35 V in this model). As VDD nears Vt, the transistors barely turn on and the delay shoots up.
Safe window
The supply range one kind of transistor can use. Too high and its thin insulation breaks down (the redline, about 1.2 V here); too low and it barely turns on (the stall, toward Vt). Going from 5 V to 1 V meant building new transistors, not turning old ones down.
Top clock, fmax
The fastest clock the slowest path allows: one clock period must cover the path's 20 gate delays, so fmax = 1 / (20 · tpd). A faster clock captures wrong values.
Dynamic power
Pdyn = α N C VDD2 f, the cost of charging and discharging every load. See Static and Dynamic Power.
Core
One complete processor inside a chip, running its own stream of instructions. A phone chip has about 6 to 8; a GPU has thousands of small ones. Each core, or group of cores, can get its own supply and clock.
DVFS
Dynamic voltage and frequency scaling: lower the supply and the clock together when full speed is not needed. This is how a phone stretches its battery.
