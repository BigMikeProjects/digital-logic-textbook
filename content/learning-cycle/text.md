# The Infobook Learning Cycle

Before we get to a single logic gate, it is worth knowing how this book expects you to use it — and
why it is built the way it is.

## Why This Book Exists

I have been studying engineering for over 48 years. When I started, learning meant reading a
textbook, going to lectures, and working problems. That was the whole toolkit.

Like most people, I have since found that I genuinely enjoy learning concepts from videos, and I
think that is a useful way to learn. But there is a problem with learning that way, and it is worth
naming: **videos tend to be disconnected topics.** Search for something and you will find a dozen
explanations of it, each excellent on its own, with no general theme organizing them into a coherent
set — and therefore **no path through the material of a course**. You end up with a pile of good
individual explanations and no sense of the shape of the subject.

In the fall of 2024, a Claude model was released that turned out to be very good at building
infographics. That sparked the idea for this book: put the video, the written explanation, and the
interactive graphic together as **three views of the same organized path**, rather than three
artifacts scattered across the internet.

## The Three Modes

The diagram on this page is the whole philosophy in one picture. At its centre is a triangle with
three corners, and you are meant to move freely among them — there is no fixed order.

**Watch.** Videos are good at building intuition and showing a concept in motion. They are usually
the fastest way into an unfamiliar idea.

**Read.** This is the step people skip, so let me argue for it directly. I have talked to students
who say, "I don't really like to read." I get it — and you do not have to like it. But reading puts
the same idea in front of you in a **different format**, and you end up **accessing the information
in a different way** than if you only watched. That difference is where a lot of the learning
actually happens.

There is also a purely practical reason. Once you have watched a video and later find yourself fuzzy
on a detail, **you do not want to sit through the whole video again** to recover one point. You want
to jump straight to the part you need. Written material is random access; video is not.

**Interact.** Many of the graphics in this book let you do something — toggle inputs, click through
examples, and watch what happens to the operations and concepts being illustrated. Some are static
diagrams, but where a concept rewards experimentation, you can experiment.

Those three modes together are **the technology part** of this course. It is the part I built.

## The Outer Loop: Internalize

Around that triangle sits a larger loop, and it is not technology at all. It is the old-fashioned
way, and it is where the learning becomes yours.

Take a concept and make sure you can:

- **Sketch the circuit.** Draw it out yourself.
- **Explain what it does on a whiteboard.** Whiteboards are great for learning, and a piece of paper
  works just as well — that is why you have a drawing template for laying out circuits, diagrams,
  and Boolean equations.
- **Talk to your classmates.** Study groups are worth your time; explaining something to someone
  else exposes the parts you only thought you understood.
- **Practice solving problems.**
- **Design new circuits**, not just trace existing ones.
- **Recall it from memory**, with nothing in front of you.

Do those and you have internalized the material — and notice that **none of them need the
technology.** That is deliberate. I want you to use the technology to help you learn, and then
**move away from it** — away from AI, away from the interactive graphics, away from all of it — and
make sure that *just you and a piece of paper*, or *just you and a whiteboard*, can explain how the
concept works.

That is the test. Not whether you followed the video, but whether you can reproduce the idea without
it.

## Mastery, Then Around Again

Getting through that outer loop is how you reach mastery on a topic. Then you take on a new topic
and run the same process again — watch, read, interact, then sketch, explain, teach, solve, design,
recall.

What happens after a few cycles is the part worth waiting for: **you start naturally making
connections** about how the world works in terms of digital logic circuits. The topics stop feeling
like a list and start feeling like a structure. I think this is a good path for learning in general,
not just for this course.

## Learn Over Time, Not the Night Before

One last thing, and it matters more here than you might expect.

It is far better to learn this material **over time** than to cram it right before an exam. You
*can* cram it — and here is the honest danger in this particular course: **the material is not
difficult enough to stop you getting away with it.** You can compress a week of digital logic into
an evening and survive the exam.

The bill comes later. You will not remember it further down the road in your engineering career,
which is precisely when you need it — because digital logic sits underneath nearly everything else
you will build.

So work the loop deliberately. There will be **reading assignments and infographic assignments
supporting every lecture**, which exist to keep you moving around this cycle rather than sprinting
through it at the end.

## Key Takeaways

This book is organized around a **learning cycle**. Inside it are three modes you move among freely
— **Watch** for intuition, **Read** for a different format and for random access to points you have
already seen, and **Interact** for experimenting with the concept directly. Those three are the
**technology** half. Around them runs the half that is not technology at all: **sketch, explain on a
whiteboard or paper, teach a classmate, solve problems, design new circuits, and recall from
memory** — the outer loop where material actually becomes yours. The deliberate instruction is to
use the technology and then **put it down**, AI included, and confirm you can work the concept with
nothing but paper. Clearing that loop is **mastery**; then the next topic goes through the same
cycle, and over time the **connections between topics form on their own**. Finally, work the cycle
**over time rather than cramming** — this course is crammable, which is exactly what makes cramming
a trap, since the cost is paid later in your career rather than on the exam.

### Question 4

Why is cramming described as an especially tempting trap in this particular course?

A. The exams are unusually difficult  
B. The material is not hard enough to stop you getting away with it, so the cost shows up later in your career  
C. There is no written material to review  
D. The topics are unrelated, so order does not matter

## Answer Explanations

**1. B.** Individual videos may be excellent, but nothing organizes them into a coherent set,
so you get a pile of explanations without the shape of the subject. Supplying that path is the
reason this book exists.

**2. C.** The same idea in a different format gives you a second way to access it, and written
material is random access — when you are fuzzy on one detail, you go straight to it instead of
sitting through the whole video again.

**3. C.** The triangle is the technology half. The outer loop is deliberately unplugged: you
sketch, explain on a whiteboard or paper, teach a classmate, solve, design, and recall from memory —
and the standard is that you can do it with nothing but paper in front of you.

**4. B.** You can compress this material and still pass the exam, which is exactly what makes
it dangerous — the failure is deferred. Digital logic sits underneath much of what you build later,
so not remembering it is a problem long after the grade is recorded.
